Dzine Review: A Great Image Editor With a Video Problem

Mar 8, 2026 by Vijay

How I tested this

I ran Dzine on the free tier — 32 credits a day, watermarked output — and spent most of that budget on the two things the platform is actually sold on: controllable image editing and image-to-video. I have not paid for a Master plan, so I can't speak to unlimited-credit workflows or the priority queue.

Everything about pricing and model lineup below was checked against Dzine's own pricing page and blog. Where I'm reporting other users' experiences rather than my own, I say so and link the source. Pricing in this category moves fast, so confirm the current numbers before you pay.

Dzine started life as Stylar, a layer-based AI image editor, and grew into video and lip-sync after a 2025 rebrand.

Most reviews of Dzine lead with the all-in-one pitch: one subscription, every AI visual tool, goodbye to your five-app stack. That's the marketing. The more useful thing to know is where the product came from — because it explains both what Dzine is unusually good at and where it falls apart.

Dzine used to be called Stylar. It launched as a layer-based AI image editor built around control — the ability to composite, mask, and steer output rather than roll the dice on a prompt and hope. In 2025 the team rebranded to Dzine and bolted on video generation and lip-sync avatars. That history is the whole story of this tool. The editing core is mature and genuinely differentiated. The video layer is newer, is mostly a wrapper around third-party models, and is where nearly every serious complaint lands.

⚡ Short version

  • Genuinely good at: controllable image editing — layers, masking, local edits, style consistency. This is the Stylar inheritance and it holds up.
  • Risky: lip-sync and precise object edits. Failed generations still burn credits, which is the complaint that comes up most.
  • Free tier: 32 credits/day, watermarked. Enough to judge the editor, not enough to judge video.
  • Paid: $8.99 Beginner, $19.99 Creator, $59.99 Master per month.
  • Skip it if: you want maximum raw image quality (Midjourney) or serious video depth (Runway, or Veo direct).

The credit system is the part to understand first

Almost everything that frustrates people about Dzine traces back to how credits work, so it's worth getting this straight before anything else.

Dzine runs one unified credit balance across image generation, video, and every other AI tool on the platform. Image credits and video credits used to be separate pools; they've been merged. In principle that's simpler and more flexible — you're not stuck holding video credits you'll never spend. In practice it means a handful of failed video attempts can eat a meaningful chunk of the same balance you were going to use for a week of image work. Top-up credits stay valid for 12 months from purchase.

Here's the part that stings: credits are consumed by generations that don't work. This isn't a hypothetical. On Trustpilot, one user described the lip-sync output putting mouth movement on both people in a two-person shot when only one was supposed to be speaking — and being charged for the unusable footage. Another reported spending an hour and a pile of credits failing to change the colour of a t-shirt. Those are the reports that should shape your expectations, because they're not about quality preferences. They're about paying for output you can't use.

Practical implication: treat video generation on Dzine as speculative spend, not a reliable production step. Test a concept at the shortest duration available before committing credits to a full clip, and don't build a client deadline around lip-sync working on the first attempt.

What the editing tools actually do well

Now the good news, and it's real. The image editing side is where Dzine's Stylar lineage shows, and it's meaningfully different from what you get in a prompt-only tool.

The core idea is layered, controllable editing. Instead of describing a complete image and accepting whatever comes back, you can composite elements, mask specific regions, and apply generation to just that region. Local edits — changing one part of an image while leaving the rest untouched — are a paid-tier feature and are the single most useful thing on the platform. Background removal is integrated rather than being a separate tool you export to. Face swap and consistent-character generation are available on Creator and above, which matters if you're producing a series of images that need the same person in them.

Style training on the Creator plan lets you train a private style and apply it across generations. For anyone producing branded content at volume, that's the feature that justifies the tier — consistency across a campaign is otherwise a genuinely hard problem with generative tools.

The tradeoff is a learning curve. The layering interface is not a one-prompt-and-done experience, and reviewers consistently note it takes time to get comfortable with. If you want to type a sentence and get a finished image, this is the wrong shape of tool and you'll find it fiddly.

A practical note on what the free tier lets you judge. Thirty-two credits a day is enough to properly exercise the editing side, and that's where you should spend them. Generate a base image, then work it: mask a region and regenerate just that part, remove the background, swap an element. The thing to pay attention to is whether the untouched areas of the image stay genuinely untouched. That's the whole promise of local editing, and it's the difference between an editor and a slot machine.

What tends to separate a good session from a frustrating one is scope. Small, well-defined edits on a clear subject behave predictably. Broad instructions across a busy composition are where results drift and credits disappear. If you're evaluating it, start with the narrow edits — if those don't hold, the ambitious ones certainly won't.

What "layered editing" actually means

This phrase gets used loosely, so it's worth pinning down — it's the concept the whole product rests on, and it explains why Dzine suits some people and frustrates others.

Most AI image tools are what you'd call single-shot. You write a prompt, the model produces a complete image, and if something's wrong your options are to rewrite the prompt and roll again, or export to Photoshop. The image arrives as one flat, indivisible thing. Iteration means regeneration, and regeneration means the parts you liked change too. Anyone who has spent twenty minutes trying to keep a good composition while fixing one bad hand knows this problem intimately.

Layered editing treats an image as separable regions you can address independently. You mask an area — the background, a garment, one object — and apply generation to only that region. Everything outside the mask is held constant. That single capability changes the working model from "describe and hope" to something closer to actual editing, where you make a change, evaluate it, and make another without losing prior progress.

The practical difference shows up in repeat work. Producing one striking image is something any decent generator does. Producing forty product shots that share a consistent look, where each needed a specific fix, is where single-shot tools collapse and layered ones earn their subscription. That's the workflow Dzine is built around, inherited directly from its Stylar origins.

The cost is conceptual overhead. You have to think in regions and layers rather than sentences, which is a design mindset rather than a prompting one. Reviewers consistently note the learning curve, and it's real. If you want to type a description and receive a finished image, this tool asks more of you than you want to give — and that's a legitimate reason to choose something else rather than a failing on your part.

A sensible way to spend your first week free

Thirty-two credits a day is enough for a genuine evaluation if you spend them deliberately rather than playing. A structure that answers the questions that matter:

Day one — baseline generation. Generate images in the style you actually need for work, not novelty prompts. You're establishing whether raw output quality clears your bar before testing anything else. If it doesn't, stop here; no amount of editing sophistication rescues a base image you don't like.

Day two — local edits. Take one image you're happy with and make three separate targeted changes to different regions. Check after each whether the rest of the image held. This is the core capability and the single most important thing to verify.

Day three — background removal and compositing. Run the task you'd actually do most often. For e-commerce that's a product isolated onto a clean background. This is the workflow most likely to justify the subscription, so test it on real assets rather than samples.

Day four — consistency. Try to produce three images that plausibly belong to the same set. Consistency is the most commonly reported weakness, so probe it directly rather than discovering it during a client job.

By day four you'll know whether the Creator tier is worth $19.99 to you, and you'll have spent nothing. Notice that this plan never tests video — deliberately. The free allowance can't support a fair video evaluation, and treating video as a bonus rather than a reason to subscribe is the correct posture with this product anyway.

Where it falls short

Four specific weak points come up repeatedly, and I'd weigh all of them before subscribing.

Output consistency is the most common complaint across the board. Not that quality is bad — that it varies between generations in ways that are hard to predict or control. For casual use that's fine. For production work where you need the fifth image to match the first, it's a real cost in retries.

Lip-sync is the weakest feature on the platform. The Trustpilot reviews on this are blunt, and the failure mode described — wrong speaker animated, credits charged anyway — is the kind of bug that makes a feature unusable for client work rather than merely imperfect.

Object removal and replacement don't reliably land. Reviewers note it works often enough to be tempting and fails often enough to be frustrating. The t-shirt colour anecdote above is the representative case: a task that sounds trivial, that the tool advertises, and that resisted an hour of attempts.

Upscaling loses fine detail. Reviewers report the upscaler is not as faithful as dedicated alternatives when preserving texture and small features.

There's also an active bug-report stream in the company's Discord community, which I'd read two ways: the product has real rough edges, and the team is at least publicly engaging with them rather than hiding.

The video question: is Dzine's video worth using?

This deserves its own section because it's the reason most people consider the platform, and the answer is more nuanced than the marketing suggests.

Dzine's video generation runs on third-party models — Kling 2.6, Seedance Pro, and Google Veo 3.1 among them. That's actually a point in its favour on quality: these are strong models, and Veo 3.1 in particular is near the front of the field. You're not getting some weak in-house model.

But it reframes what you're buying. You're paying Dzine for convenient access to models you could reach elsewhere, wrapped in a workspace where your generated images are already sitting. If that convenience is worth it to you, fine — that's a legitimate value proposition. If you mainly want Veo output and you're comfortable going direct, the wrapper is adding a credit system and a failure mode between you and the model.

Where the wrapper genuinely earns its keep is the image-to-video path. Generate an image in Dzine, refine it with local edits, animate it — without exporting anything. For social content that starts from a still, that's a real workflow advantage.

How it compares to what you might already be paying for

Dzine is rarely a straight replacement for a specialist tool. It's usually a question of whether breadth beats depth for your particular work.

If you currently use Does Dzine replace it?
Midjourney (raw image quality) No. Midjourney's ceiling on pure generative quality is higher. Dzine wins on editing control, not on the image itself.
Runway (video production) No. Runway has far more depth in video tooling. Dzine's video is convenience access to third-party models.
Canva (templates and layout) Partly. Different premise — Canva is template-first, Dzine is generation-first. Canva is easier; Dzine is more capable generatively.
Photoshop (precise editing) No, but it dents it. Local edits and background removal cover common tasks faster. Precision work still goes to Photoshop.
A standalone background remover Yes. This is the clearest cancellation on the list.

Who should actually use it

Good fit: e-commerce sellers who need product images composited onto clean or lifestyle backgrounds at volume — the layered editing and background removal are directly aimed at this. Social media managers producing branded image series where style training pays for itself. Anyone currently paying separately for a background remover plus an image generator.

Poor fit: anyone whose output quality is their competitive edge in a single medium — you'll want the specialist. Anyone who needs reliable lip-sync or talking-head video; that's the weakest thing here and there are better dedicated tools. Anyone who wants prompt-and-done simplicity, because the layering interface will feel like work.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Dzine the same as Stylar?

Yes. Stylar rebranded to Dzine in 2025 when the platform expanded past image editing into video and lip-sync. Same team, same layer-based editing core. If you find older Stylar tutorials, the editing concepts still apply.

Do failed generations use up credits?

Yes, and this is the most important thing to know before subscribing. Multiple users have reported being charged for output that was unusable, particularly with lip-sync. Budget for retries and test cheaply before committing credits to a final render.

Is the free plan enough to evaluate it?

For the image editing tools, yes — 32 daily credits is enough to form a real opinion, though everything is watermarked. For video, no. Video generations cost enough that you'll exhaust a day's allowance quickly, and if a couple of attempts fail you've learned nothing.

Which video models does Dzine use?

Kling 2.6, Seedance Pro, and Google Veo 3.1, among others. These are genuinely capable third-party models rather than a weaker in-house one — but it does mean you're paying for convenient access rather than something exclusive to the platform.

Which plan should I pick?

Creator at $19.99 for most people, because private style training and consistent characters are what make it useful for repeat branded work. Beginner at $8.99 if you only need the editor without watermarks. Master at $59.99 only makes sense at genuine agency volume.

Verdict

🏆 Our Assessment: Dzine

A strong controllable image editor with a video layer bolted on that hasn't caught up yet. Buy it for the editing — layers, local edits, style training, background removal — and treat the video as a bonus that sometimes works. Buying it primarily for lip-sync or reliable video output is the mistake to avoid.

The all-in-one framing oversells it. Dzine won't replace Midjourney's image ceiling or Runway's video depth, and pretending otherwise sets you up for disappointment. What it will replace is a background removal subscription and a lot of exporting between apps — and for people doing high-volume product or branded imagery, that's worth $20 a month on its own.

My honest recommendation: spend a week on the free tier working only the editing tools. If the layered approach clicks for you, Creator is good value. If it feels fiddly, no amount of feature breadth will fix that, and you should go buy the specialist tool for whatever you actually do most.
