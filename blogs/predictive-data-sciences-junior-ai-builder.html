What This Role Is Actually About

Predictive Data Sciences, operating under the Blue Sherpa brand, builds AI-powered systems for lenders and credit funds. Their goal is to help risk teams move faster — from raw signal to decision — using intelligent automation, document understanding, and AI-assisted workflows. That business context matters a lot when you think about what this role actually involves.

This is not a company experimenting with AI to look good in investor decks. They are building systems that directly influence how financial professionals assess risk, review credit applications, and process underwriting documents. That means the quality of the AI systems you build here has measurable business consequences — which is both exciting and motivating for anyone serious about applied AI work.

For a fresher, that context translates into one key advantage: you get to work on real problems, not dummy datasets or internal hackathon projects. The role is positioned as an entry point for applied AI engineering, where you will contribute to building, testing, and improving LLM-based workflows that real analysts and risk teams depend on.

The hiring note from the team also makes something very clear: they want builders, not buzzword enthusiasts. If your idea of an AI job is clicking together no-code blocks or writing a one-line prompt and calling it a day, this role is not for you. But if you enjoy the actual work of making AI systems reliable, measurable, and useful — this is exactly where you want to start your career.

Day-to-Day Responsibilities

One of the most practical things to understand before applying for any role is what your actual working day looks like. Here is a realistic breakdown of what Junior AI Builders at this type of company work on:

  • Building document processing pipelines: Think PDF credit reports, financial statements, underwriting memos. You help turn these into structured data through LLM extraction, validation, and schema enforcement.
  • Designing and refining prompts for production use: Not one-off prompts — but carefully structured prompts with output contracts, guardrails, and fallback handling that hold up under varied input.
  • Setting up and running retrieval-augmented workflows: You build the pipeline that lets the AI system find the right context from large document sets before generating an answer or summary.
  • Evaluation and quality measurement: Defining what "good output" means, writing test cases, running systematic evals, and tracking failure rates across different scenarios.
  • Debugging system failures: This is a big part of the job. When the system gives a wrong answer, extracts the wrong field, or produces an unusable output — you investigate why and fix it methodically.
  • Collaborating with experienced practitioners: You will not be dropped into the deep end alone. Senior team members work alongside you, especially on ambiguous problems or new system designs.
  • Translating business requirements into technical specs: Risk analysts may describe a problem in business language. Part of your job is turning that into a clear first technical implementation.
  • Iterating based on user feedback: Building the first version is only step one. You refine, improve, and re-test until the system works reliably for real users.

The pattern here is clear: this is incremental, evidence-driven, practical work. The kind of work that actually teaches you how production AI systems are built and maintained — which is far more valuable than theoretical knowledge alone.

Skills You Need to Get Shortlisted

The job listing identifies two non-negotiable foundations: strong Python coding and meaningful hands-on exposure to the modern AI stack. Let us translate those into specific, actionable skill checkpoints.

Python Proficiency That Goes Beyond Syntax

Recruiters and interviewers at applied AI roles are not just checking whether you can write Python — they are checking whether you can write good Python. That means code that is modular, readable, testable, and maintainable. During interviews, expect to be evaluated on how you structure a project, not just whether you can get it to run once.

Specifically, get comfortable with: writing clean functions with single responsibilities, organizing code into logical modules, using type hints, handling exceptions gracefully, and writing basic unit tests. These habits signal professional readiness, even at 0 experience.

Hands-On AI Stack Knowledge

The listing explicitly calls out these areas — and you should be able to speak from real project experience for at least a few of them:

  • LLM APIs and workflow construction — using OpenAI, Anthropic, or open-source model APIs to build actual workflows, not just make a single API call
  • Prompt engineering and structured outputs — designing prompts that reliably return JSON, lists, or structured data; handling edge cases when they do not
  • Retrieval and embeddings — building RAG pipelines, working with vector stores, understanding chunking strategies and context window trade-offs
  • Evaluation design — defining test cases, measuring accuracy or reliability, and using eval results to drive improvements
  • Fine-tuning basics — understanding when fine-tuning makes sense versus prompting; practical experience is a bonus but not a hard requirement
  • Tool-use and agent-style patterns — building workflows where the LLM calls tools, routes decisions, or handles multi-step tasks

A Builder's Mindset and Independent Drive

The role description repeatedly emphasizes the ability to make progress independently and build things outside of formal assignments. This is a genuine screening factor. Teams at this scale do not have time to hand-hold every decision — they need junior members who ask good questions, make reasonable choices, and come back with something to show.

Debugging Instinct

Being able to diagnose what went wrong — and fix it systematically — is one of the most underrated skills in AI engineering. Many freshers can build a working demo. Fewer can explain why it broke in production and what they changed to fix it. That ability to articulate failure and recovery is exactly what experienced teams look for when hiring someone with limited work history.

A 10-Day Interview Preparation Plan

If you are applying now and want to be genuinely ready within two weeks, here is a practical day-by-day framework:

Days 1–2: Build a Clean Mini LLM Workflow

Create a small but complete project — something with user input, retrieval from a small document set, structured JSON output, basic validation logic, and simple error logging. The goal is not to build something impressive; it is to build something explainable. You should be able to walk through every part of it in an interview without hesitation.

Days 3–4: Add Evals and a Failure Log

Write explicit test cases for your workflow. Then deliberately break it — try edge-case inputs, ambiguous queries, missing data. Document where it fails and what you changed to improve it. This gives you a genuine debugging and evaluation story to tell, which directly maps to one of the core role expectations.

Day 5: Code Quality Pass

Refactor your project. Break long functions apart, add meaningful variable names, write at least a few unit tests, and clean up any duplicated logic. Interviewers who look at your code notice structure as much as functionality — clean code signals professional readiness.

Day 6: Retrieval and Embeddings Deep Dive

Spend a focused day on retrieval mechanics. Understand different chunking strategies and their trade-offs, how vector similarity search works at a conceptual level, what reranking does and when it helps, and common reasons why RAG systems return irrelevant context. Being able to discuss these trade-offs confidently is a strong interview signal.

Day 7: API and Deployment Basics

Expose one of your workflows through a simple FastAPI or Flask endpoint. Test it with sample request payloads. This demonstrates that you understand how AI systems connect to the rest of an application stack — not just how to run them in a notebook.

Day 8: Systematic Debugging Drill

Simulate specific failure types: token limit exceeded, malformed JSON output, retrieval returning wrong context, prompt producing inconsistent results across runs. Practice walking through each failure methodically — what you observed, what you suspected, what you checked, and what you changed. This is the kind of structured debugging thinking that applied AI teams value.

Day 9: Business Communication Practice

Explain your project to someone outside tech — a friend, a family member, anyone without a CS background. Then explain it to a technical interviewer with a focus on trade-offs and decisions. Being able to code-switch between these two audiences is a skill that matters from day one in a role like this.

Day 10: Mock Rounds and Resume Finalization

Do one timed coding exercise on arrays, strings, or basic algorithms. Run through a system design walkthrough of your project out loud. Practice one behavioral scenario focused on how you handled ambiguity or learned something independently. Then finalize your resume with fresh eyes.

Common Challenges and How to Handle Them

Every role that involves real building comes with real friction. Here are the challenges freshers most commonly encounter in applied AI engineering roles — and how to approach them:

Challenge: Ambiguous Requirements

Stakeholders in financial teams often describe problems in business language that does not translate directly into a technical specification. The mistake most freshers make is waiting for a perfect spec before starting. A better approach: ask two or three clarifying questions to understand the outcome the stakeholder cares about, then build the simplest version that addresses that outcome and show it quickly. Feedback on a running prototype is far more useful than extended back-and-forth on a written spec.

Challenge: AI Systems That Are Unpredictable

LLMs are non-deterministic, which means the same input can produce different outputs across runs. This feels uncomfortable at first, especially when you are used to deterministic code. The solution is not to fight this — it is to build evals that tell you how often the system produces acceptable output, and to set reliability thresholds that match the business use case. Some variability is fine; some is not. Knowing the difference is the skill.

Challenge: Debugging Without Obvious Error Messages

Software bugs throw exceptions. AI system failures often look like quietly wrong answers. Developing a systematic debugging habit — logging intermediate outputs, testing with controlled inputs, comparing expected versus actual outputs across a test suite — is what transforms a frustrating debugging session into a productive learning process.

Challenge: Imposter Syndrome in a Team of Senior Practitioners

Working alongside more experienced engineers can feel intimidating. The healthiest mindset here is to treat every interaction as a learning opportunity rather than an evaluation. Ask questions when you are unsure. Own your mistakes clearly and describe what you learned. Senior engineers on good teams respect intellectual honesty far more than the appearance of knowing everything.

Challenge: Keeping Up with a Fast-Moving Field

New models, new frameworks, and new techniques emerge constantly in AI. The risk is spending so much time staying current that you never build depth in any one area. A practical approach: maintain a consistent reading habit — two or three relevant articles per week — but prioritize depth in your core skill set over breadth across every new trend.
